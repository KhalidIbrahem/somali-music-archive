## Key question 1: Genres and forms (qaraami, balwo, heello, hees types, dances, poetry genres, religious repertoires)

### Takeaway
The best-documented picture (Giannattasio 1988; Xaange 2014; Johnson via Wikipedia) divides Somali verbal-musical practice into gabay (recited poetry, by extension all the *maanso* genres), hees (sung folk and modern song) and ciyaar (dance, including possession rituals), with *digri* (dhikr) outside "music"; balwo (1943, Cabdi Deeqsi "Sinimo") gave rise to the heello with the kaban (late 1940s), and "qaraami" is a later umbrella word for that classic repertoire. Dates and origins of dhaanto, balwo and the kaban's arrival are each given differently by different sources and must be flagged as disputed.

### Cited Findings

**Classification and the maanso–hees line**
- Giannattasio's field survey (Nov 1984 onward, with National Theatre director Aweys Maxamed "Waasuge") distinguishes three performing practices: *gabyid* (to compose and perform poetry), *heesid* (to sing songs) and *ciyaarid* (to play-dance); "gabay" names the chief poetic genre but by extension all traditional poetic genres "from gabay to buraanbur (female poetry) to masafo (religious poetry)"; *hees* covers folk songs of the life cycle (lullabies, *hoobeeya*; working songs, *heeso howleed*) plus the "minor" genres *wiglo, hirwo, dhaanto, belwo* (Johnson's "Family of Miniature Genres") and the new national song *heello*; *ciyaar* covers dance music of the ludic kind (*beerrey, bullo* etc.) and of possession ritual (*saar*) — [Giannattasio 1988, pp. 161–163](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984) (PDF: /bitstream/2307/984/5/).
- Giannattasio's Fig. 1 lists: GABAY — gabay, geeraar, guurow, buraanbur, jiifto, masafo; HEES — "Hoobeeyaa", h. carruureed, h. howleed (a) xoolaad, b) kebdeed, c) beereed, d) badeed), and the bridge group wiglo, hirwo, dhaanto, belwo, heello; CIYAAR — beerrey, kabeebey, bullo, shirib, and Saar: a) saar, b) nuumbi, c) mingis, d) xayaad; DIGRI set apart — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 163](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984).
- The chants of the *digri* (dhikr), i.e. "the intoned and rhythmically cadenced recitation of prayers" of the Islamic orders, are "completely extraneous from the idea of music": for *qasiidooyin* one may not use the verbs *qaadid* (to sing/perform a known song) or *heesid* — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 162](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984).
- Hirsi (2023): "The maanso genres of poetry (gabay, geeraar, masafo) are often seen as the preeminent form of poetry in nomadic pastoralist poetic culture ... as opposed to the hees which were light poetry forms that accompanied work as work songs and dances as dance songs"; southern (Banaadiri) poetry differs markedly in genre and form — [Hirsi 2023, WardheerNews PDF](https://wardheernews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Aweys-Geedow-Nuur-Diinles-Ciyow-Biyaha-Keen-A-Case-Study-By-Ibrahim-Hirsi.pdf).
- Kapteijns glosses *hees* as the Somali term for "modern song", including love songs and political songs, with *waddani* ("patriotic") songs as a sub-genre of the political ones; the state promoted a national popular culture through the radio stations of Mogadishu and Hargeisa and the National Theatre and put many artists on its payroll — [Kapteijns 2009, J. African History 50(1):101–122](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/discourse-on-moral-womanhood-in-somali-popular-songs-19601990/8B56054C3458B9805AA848CCC8079B06).
- Orwin's 2021 article "Lyric voice in hees and maanso" exists (unora.unior.it PDF returned HTTP 403; not read) — [search result](https://unora.unior.it/retrieve/dfd1bedd-aa05-d55a-e053-3705fe0af723/Orwin-2021-Lyric_voice_in_hees_and_maanso.pdf).
- Syllable counts per line attributed to Galaal (1970) on Wikipedia: gabay 14–16; geeraar 7; jiifto 7; halaanhal ("oldest metre") 12; heello 10; hees-xoolaad ("livestock song") 4–5; hees-cayaareed ("dance song") 6–11; hees-caanood ("milk-shaking song") 6–9; hees-mooye ("grinding song") 6; hees-carruureed ("children's song") 8 — [Wikipedia, Somali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_literature) (these are syllable counts from an older analysis; Banti & Giannattasio and Orwin describe the metres as quantitative/moraic, see KQ3).

**Qaraami**
- The Giannattasio catalogue records (30 Apr 1985, singer Xaaji "Baalbaal") a piece labelled "'Qaraami', tipo di gabay (genere poetico) ad argomento amoroso" ("a type of gabay on a love theme"), opening with the stereotyped line "Hoobaalayeey hobaalayeey hobaalayeey hobeey" — [Catalogo dei documenti sonori, DAT 7 id 14](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Jama Musse Jama's *Qaraami: The Fading Melody of Somali Classic Music* (Ponte Invisibile/Redsea, 2023, 234 pp.) says "The etymology of the word is qaram, from the Arabic word love", that the term covers Somaliland's oldest modern music "mainly from the 1940s to the mid-1960s", later meaning "old-time good music", and contains first transcriptions of the melodies Beerdillaacshe, Carwo, Murug, Nugul and Aroor — [African Books Collective](https://www.africanbookscollective.com/books/qaraami); [Savvy Contemporary launch, 4 Nov 2023](https://savvy-contemporary.com/en/events/2023/qaraami/) (spells the root "garam"). Neither gives the Arabic spelling; no dictionary was fetched.
- Geeska: "Qaraam" emerged between the 1940s and 1960s fusing oral poetry with structured melodies and instrumental accompaniment; "qaraami", derived from it, is now an everyday term for "old Somali songs", often any 1950s–1970s music — [Geeska, "The making of Hudeidi"](https://www.geeska.com/en/making-hudeidi).
- Harvard Loeb: *qaraami* = love songs, which "can also express love of family, nature, or God"; Hudeidi studied Arabic *maqamat* and *taqasim* in Aden — [Loeb Music blog, Hudeidi post](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/loebmusic/2021/05/06/hudeidi-the-somali-king-of-oud-another-sad-casualty-of-covid-19/).
- Wikipedia's Qaraami article (citing Afrax 2013 SOAS thesis, Plastow 2020, Woolner 2021, C. A. Farah 2018) says the genre emerged from a 1930s poetry form of the same name originating in Saylac; "Kabacad" youth (white shoes) sang it at weddings and *gaaf* gatherings; poets composed in a circle by *subcis* (rotation) in *lug-kowle* (one-line), *labaale* (couplet) and *saddexle* (triplet) formats, the last half-line (*hooris*) chanted by all; Qarshe is credited as first to set qaraami poetry to music (Woolner 2021 p. 283); the "Dhaanto influence" and "Qaraami 1/2" claims are uncited — [Wikipedia, Qaraami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaraami).
- Bashir Goth (Music in Africa): *dunya diwani* ("the world is my poetry book"), Arabic songs from Aden played on phonographs, dominated 1939–43 until balwo replaced the Arabic words with Somali; "kabacad" ("clean shoes") were the groups who played them; qaraami is "a generic name for classic Somali music, mostly performed with oud and drum", initiated by Walaalo Hargeisa — [Music in Africa, "Somali music through the ages"](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169).
- The Ostinato compilation's track 7 is Xasan Diiriye, "Qaraami (Love)" — [Ostinato Bandcamp](https://ostinatorecords.bandcamp.com/album/sweet-as-broken-dates-lost-somali-tapes-from-the-horn-of-africa).

**Balwo / belwo**
- Giannattasio catalogue, recording Qarshe on 15 Jan 1985: "Belwo (genere creato nel 1943 da Cabdi Shineemo) — 'Lay lay' (segna il passaggio dal belwo al nuovo genere heello)" — belwo created 1943 by Cabdi "Shineemo"; "Lay lay" marks the passage from belwo to heello; performed with voice, kaban, violin, flute, bongos, *daf* and handclaps — [Catalogo, DAT 3 id 1](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Wikipedia Balwo (citing Johnson 1996; Mukhtar 2003; Africa Diary 1964): the name "means 'misfortune'"; founder Abdi Sinimo (Cabdi Deeqsi, Reer Nuur/Gadabuursi), born Jarahorato near Borama; the first balwo was improvised when his lorry broke down near Zeila, dated "between 1943 and 1945" in one section and 1945 in another (internal inconsistency); religious leaders composed poems against it; heello is its immediate successor; a four-line sample is given — [Wikipedia, Balwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balwo).
- Goth: Abdi Sinimo "sings the first modern Somali song note" in 1943; first Balwo Band formed in Borama 1944; the second band shifted balwo from love to patriotism; the third Balwo Band was the first to use the oud and disbanded 1946; his first sound came from beating an empty kerosene tin; Khadiija Balwo (Khadiija Ciye Dharaar) was his partner and among the first Somali women performers; Hajji Manati was a religious critic — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169).
- Wikipedia Somali literature (citing Johnson 1996 / Mukhtar 2003) instead says balwo "developed in the 1920s and reached its pinnacle during the 1940s" — contradicted by the 1943 dating above — [Wikipedia, Somali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_literature).
- Xaaji Baalbaal (1985) presented "Dheel", a danced song he connected to the dhaanto "jiibdheer" "from which the balwo genre is said to derive", and a balwo opening with the stereotyped "Balwooy balwooy hooy balwooy" — [Catalogo, DAT 7 ids 24–25](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Radio Hargeisa "helped spread the Balwo genre ... [and] the Heellooy oud music that Abdullahi Qarshe blended with Balwo" — [Wikipedia, Radio Hargeysa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Hargeysa).

**Heello / heellooy**
- Giannattasio: the new urban music "owes much to the adoption of the Yemenite ... lute (kaban) which gave a new musical structure to the metrical pattern of some 'minor' poetical genres (hirwo, dhaanto, wiglo, belwo) and thus gave birth to a new genre called heello. The new Somali song has evolved from the heello" — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 161](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984); the Italian 1987 chapter dates the kaban's introduction to 1948 — [Giannattasio 1987, p. 75](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736).
- Qarshe's political heello "Hubiyaa Harar iyo Hawaasta Galbeed / Hadmey isu hiilin doonaaney?" was "composto nel 1949"; the catalogue calls Qarshe (1924–1997?) "massimo esponente della nuova canzone (heello) e fra i maggiori autori della canzone politica somala" — [Catalogo, DAT 2 ids 8–12](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Wikipedia Somali literature (citing Johnson): "Heellooy" was initially "a long series of short Balwo", performed with oud and drums, later linked into "one long continuous song"; later enhanced by Cumar Dhuule, Mohamed Mooge, Ahmed Naaji, Mohamed Sulayman Tubeec — [Wikipedia, Somali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_literature).
- Johnson's 1974 Indiana dissertation/book is titled *Heellooy Heelleellooy: The Development of the Genre Heello in Modern Somali Poetry*; the 1996 edition is *Heelloy: Modern Poetry and Songs of the Somali* (ISBN 978-1874209812); Giannattasio notes Johnson's own doubt "as to whether they are songs or poems" and that the miniature genres sit "astride" gabay and hees — [Giannattasio 1988, n. 9](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984); [British Museum library record](https://library.britishmuseum.org/bib/151187).
- Guduuda 'Arwo (Shamis Abokor Ismail, b. 1935, d. 2017) recorded her first *heello* for Radio Hargeisa in August 1953 under a false name, claimed as the first female recording vocalist in the Protectorate (claim not independently sourced on the page) — [Wikipedia, Guduuda 'Arwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guduuda_%27Arwo).
- Goth: heello is "a more modernised version of balwo", associated with the patriotic songs of the independence struggle, using patterned refrains sung by male and female choirs — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169).
- Xaange (1991 introduction) excludes "the spiritual and the modern lyric (Heello) songs" from his folk-song collection — [Xaange 2014, p. 14](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).

**Wiglo, hirwo/hurbo, dhaanto, dheel, haan, batar, baarcadde (Xaange and Giannattasio)**
- *Wilgo/Wiglo*: "one of the oldest genres in Somali popular songs", originated by Somali sailors on dhows and spread among nomads; two-line verses sung by a soloist or with a chorus repeating refrain phrases such as "woyaale woyaale woyaaley / woyaale kalaynu leenahayey!" (meaningless vocables giving the singer time to prepare the next line); "no musical accompaniments of any sort" — [Xaange 2014, ch. IV §1](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).
- *Hurbo/Hirwo*: "mainly in the 1950s" the wilgo style spread in the western regions and "became a dancing song" renamed hurbo/hirwo, adding hand-clapping and foot-stamping; two short lines per verse; composed spontaneously by both sexes while dancing; the informant's sequence "Wilgo Wilgo Wilgo / Hirwo Hirwo / Dhaanto..." — [Xaange 2014, ch. IV §2](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf). Giannattasio's catalogue has Xaaji Baalbaal label "Hurbo" the poetic genre "from which the dhaanto genre called 'jiibdheer' is said to derive" and "xurbo" (lit. "mixed", between dhaanto and hirwo) — [Catalogo, DAT 4 id 23; DAT 7 id 23](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- *Dhaanto*: per Xaange's informant it "developed from the Hurbo" and "first appeared in the '30s of the last century as a dancing song among the Somali pastoralists, especially the camel-herders in the western part of the country", then spread to towns; two-line verses with their own theme; "Wilgo and Dhaanto verses could, in fact, be sung interchangeably; only their melodies vary"; a chorus provides "energetic hand-clapping and foot-stamping" — [Xaange 2014, ch. IV §3](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).
- Giannattasio recorded in Jigjiga (24 Sep 1998) "Dhaanto, genere poetico moderno" in four "tipi", type 1 "corrispondente alla prima delle quattro fasi in cui si articola una seduta di dhaanto" (first of the four phases of a dhaanto session), performed by six mixed voices with a plastic jerrycan drum and handclaps; the catalogue also gives dhaanto as the genre of singers' self-presentation couplets (laashinno, heesaayaal: Dhugdhugley, Meytinaar, Cawdubille, Baalbaal) — [Catalogo, DAT 9 ids 30–37; DAT 7 id 19](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf); three dhaanto salutation couplets are transcribed in 2/8 and 3/8 — [Giannattasio 1988, pp. 166–167](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984).
- Wikipedia Dhaanto (citing Giannattasio 1988, Koskoff 2008, Johnson 1974 p. 30 and 1996 p. 28): native to the Somali Region of Ethiopia; "revived in the late 19th century by the Dervish Movement", used to raise soldiers' spirits and "often sung on horseback"; the "invented by the Ogaden clan" sentence is uncited; the page is a stub with nothing on rhythm — [Wikipedia, Dhaanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhaanto).
- Dur-Dur Band Int.: the groove is "strongly accented on the off-beat"; listeners think of reggae but the musicians insist "the style is Dhaanto ... our rhythm"; it "originated in the Ogaden region" in the era of "clap & chant"; some say it imitates the bounce of a camel; *kooxda* = band — [Dur-Dur Band Int., Bandcamp](https://dur-durbandint.bandcamp.com).
- A Substack essay credits Cabdillaahi Xirsi "Baarleex" (Galbeed) with a 1987 Mogadishu festival performance that made dhaanto national, with solo/male-and-female-choir refrains and changing refrains; its only non-YouTube source is a mention in the *Concise Garland Encyclopedia*; treat as unverified — [alxnur.substack.com](https://alxnur.substack.com/p/the-father-of-dhaanto-cabdillaahi).
- Twin Cities PBS (Somali Museum of Minnesota, 2018): dhaanto "is inspired by the camel"; jaandheer means "big leap"/"big step", danced at weddings with a dizzying spinning move; saylici ("Syalici") originated in Zeila as a religious chant that became a dance — [TPT, "Somali Traditional Dance 101"](https://www.tptoriginals.org/somali-traditional-dance-101/).
- *Haan* (dance): "very ancient folk songs performed solely by women ... also called durbaan", popular in Mudug, Nugaal, Sanaag; four or five young women; an empty *haan* milk vessel with a wet kid skin stretched over its mouth is used as a drum; men may only watch — [Xaange 2014, ch. IV §4](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf). Giannattasio (Jigjiga 1998): "Haan: danza mf della stagione fertile, spesso con trance (in altre regioni detta anche Saar)" — a mixed-sex dance of the fertile season, often with trance, "in other regions also called Saar", normally performed with mixed voices — [Catalogo, DAT 9 id 20](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- *Batar (Sacab)*: a "nationwide" dance "commonly performed in the central, eastern, northern and southern regions ... as well as in most of the urban centres", absent only in the west where dhaanto and hurbo dominate; technique like dhaanto but "synchronized to a different rhythm pattern": "ta-tam-ta-tam-ta-tam-ta-tam ta-ta-tam..."; staged at the coming of the rains — [Xaange 2014, ch. IV §5](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf). Giannattasio: "'Batar', canto di danza (cayaar), zona di Ceerigaabo (Sanaag)", voice + handclaps + foot stamped on the ground — [Catalogo, DAT 7 id 17](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- *Shirib*: "'Shirib', canto di danza, nello stile della regione Sanaag (e del Bari)", incipit "Tuma dheh, taa / Cusub dheh" — [Catalogo, DAT 7 id 18](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf); the same song ("Beat, that new one (dance)...") is transcribed as Example 1, a dance fragment from the Ceerigaabo region at ♪ ≈ 205 — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 165](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984). In the south, Hirsi describes *shirib* as "a particular type of popular southern" form used for verses or bridges in Banaadiri songs ("if the bridge is a shirib, all bridges within the song will be shirib") — [Hirsi 2023](https://wardheernews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Aweys-Geedow-Nuur-Diinles-Ciyow-Biyaha-Keen-A-Case-Study-By-Ibrahim-Hirsi.pdf); and Giannattasio 1987 says the conch trumpet is used "per marcare i passaggi di una particolare forma di canto, lo *shirib*, e, fra gli agricoltori, per chiamare all'aiuto reciproco" — [Giannattasio 1987, p. 81](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736).
- *Baarcadde*: a dance style born from memorial songs for the legendary itinerant singer-drummer Bile — [Xaange 2014, ch. IV §6](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).
- Southern/Banaadiri and riverine dances recorded by the National Theatre folklore department (31 Jan 1985) and at Far Xaano near Baraawe: "Sayle", "Beerrey" (whistles, *sharaq* metal clappers, *sacab*), "Seylici" (handclaps + one drum), "Buunlo" (*jabbu* drum, *buun*, *sharaq*, *malkad*, *baaf*, *sacab*, foot stamping *lugo*), "Madina" (*moqoddon* and *jabbu* drums, *sharaq*), "Kabeebey" (ululation *alalas*, drums, *baaf*, claps, stamping; at Far Xaano with a metal sheet struck with sticks) — [Catalogo, DAT 4 ids 12–19; DAT 5 ids 3–5](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf). Giannattasio 1987 adds that *sharaq* are used in "Bullo, Gebley Shimbir, Beerrey" and *shanbal* in the *xayaad* dance — [Giannattasio 1987, p. 79](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736).
- Goth lists the pre-1960s dances "xoogweyn, saddexley, dhaanto, hirwo, wilwile, saylici", women's light dance songs *heelo-yar-yar*, lullabies *hees carruureed* and work songs *hees-hawleed* — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169). A Minneapolis report describes *saylici* as a Sufi-inspired dance of the Samaroon/Gadabuursi and *wilwile* as an Issa warrior dance (search snippet only) — [Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/somali-museum-dance-troupe-minneapolis/).

**Work songs, lullabies and women's genres**
- Catalogue genre labels (Baabile/Jigjiga 1998; Mogadishu 1985): *hees-kebdeed* or *heesta kebedda* (weaving the *kebed* mat of the *aqal*); *hees-ari* (herding goats and sheep); *hees-carruureed* (lullaby, incipits "Shubeeyaa shubeey", "Hoobeey hoobeeya", "Hobeey Hobeeyaa, Hooyo hobaale"); *hees-maqaleed / heesta maqasha* (herding kids); *hees-alool* (building the stick bed-frame); *heesta caano-ruxidda* (butter churning, presented locally as *heesta culidda haana*); *hees-geel* (camel watering, "tipico dei Daarood Ogaadeen, reg. del Doollo"); *hees-rakaadeed* (second watering, *rakaad*); *hees-beerta* (sowing); *salsal* (loading the pack-saddle); *heesta dibida* (ploughing oxen, with a 1960 verse against tractors at Jigjiga); *hees fardood* (horses); *hees baddeed* / *hees-badda* (fishermen's songs, Jasiira); *hees doonnieed* (dhow song); *qasiido* for *Mawliid* — [Catalogo, DAT 5 id 13; DAT 7 ids 3–16; DAT 9 ids 8–38](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Xaange's 191 songs (Mudug, Nugaal, Sanaag; collected over ~20 years, intro dated Mogadishu 10 Sep 1991) are classed as nursery songs; work songs of home-making (*kebed* weaving, *raar* grass-mat, *haan* churning); livestock songs (camels, loading, cattle, sheep, goats, horses, rain songs); entertainment songs (wilgo, hurbo, dhaanto, haan, batar, baarcadde); animal songs; nursery songs are called *hobeeya hobeeya*, or *huwaaya huwaaya* in southern dialect, vocables meaning roughly "Hey! Hey!" or "Shssh!"; the buraanbur line has two hemistichs whereas the nursery-song line has one — [Xaange 2014, pp. 13–14, 19–20, 33](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).
- Wikipedia Buraanbur: "the female counterpart to the gabay ... a lighter measure (miisaan)" (Abdullahi 2001); "the highest women's literary genre ... subdivided into the hoyal, hoobeeyo and sitaat (religious song)" (Wieringa 1995); accompanied by drums, clapping, dancing; poets Halima Godane, Raha Ayaanle Guled, Hawa Jibril — [Wikipedia, Buraanbur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buraanbur). Giannattasio recorded buraambur by seven girls in Baabile with a plastic-jerrycan drum, handclaps and *alalaas* (ululation) — [Catalogo, DAT 9 ids 2–6](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Hirsi documents the Banaadiri fishing couplet *dooro duureed aa* (lead singer sings the *hoojis*, the rest sing the *hooris* in unison), used by Aweys Geedow Nuur Diinle (1925–1970, "father of Banaadiri theatre") in "Ciyow Biyaha Keen" (1968) from the play *Talo Xumo Tol Ma Badiyo*, "the first Banaadiri production to be performed at the newly-built National Theatre and only the second play to be showcased there, the first being Shabeelnaagood"; geeraar couplets (*geeraaro*) served as the dialogue medium in Banaadiri plays — [Hirsi 2023](https://wardheernews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Aweys-Geedow-Nuur-Diinles-Ciyow-Biyaha-Keen-A-Case-Study-By-Ibrahim-Hirsi.pdf).

**Religious and ritual repertoires**
- *Mingis* is "variante migiurtina del saar (zar) somalo" (the Majeerteen/north-eastern variant of Somali saar), led by a *calaqad* (priest), here Jaamac Waledi, with a suite of songs "in cryptic language to invoke the jinn", 3 drums + 1 metal drum (*teneg*) + mixed voices + hands; recorded 11 Mar 1982 at Madiina, Mogadishu — [Catalogo, DAT 1–2](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf); analysed in Giannattasio, "Somalia: la terapia coreutico-musicale del Mingis", *Culture musicali* II(3), 1983, pp. 93–119 — [ArcAdiA record](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/2045); a video record describes mingis as "a therapeutic rite ... to cure possession, traditionally practiced among Somali shepherds", the *calaqad/calawaad* directing it — [ArcAdiA 2307/2128](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/2128).
- *Nuumbi* (var. *luumbi*): possession cult of the Shabeelle farmers (Raxanweyn Geledi, Lama-Doonka; Buulo Sheekh; Far Xaano), songs in a cryptic language, accompanied by the *shareero* (six-string lyre, also called *lumiya*), three *tabuul* drums and handclaps, with an officiant (*Naa'ib tumburi*) — [Catalogo, DAT 1, DAT 3 ids 8–13, DAT 5 ids 9–12](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- *Xayaad*: a religious ritual (recorded at the *siyaaro* for Sheekh Xasan Buraale, Jasiira, 4 May 1985) with *qasiidooyin* in Arabic and Somali, *shanbal* clappers and two *owlan* kettledrums, with women in trance — [Catalogo, DAT 8](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf); Giannattasio lists *boorane* and *xayaad* as possession forms undergoing "Islamisation" — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 162](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984).
- *Digri* (dhikr) of the *dariiqo/tariiqo* Qaadiriya, Xuseeniya, Axmadiya, Makhtuubiya (a women's congregation of the Qaadiriya) and Seyliciya, at *siyaaro* commemorations: *qasiidooyin* in Arabic, Somali and Maay; women's digri "Abbaay abbaay" / "Abbaay Faadumo"; the *khaliif* gives the blessing (*duco*) and chants the *madded* invocation to the saints (*owliyo*); *Nebi-ammaan* (songs in praise of the Prophet) sung at a *leyli* (evening session) for a funeral commemoration (*xus*) by a sheekh and pious women; *nasar* drum and *sacab* in Seyliciya digri — [Catalogo, DAT 4 ids 1–11, DAT 5–7](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- *Sittaat/sitaat/sitaad*: Kapteijns & Maryan Omar Ali, "Sittaat: Somali Women's Songs for 'The Mothers of the Believers'" (Boston University African Studies Center working paper 1995; also in *The Marabout and the Muse*, 1996) — [BU IJAHS listing](https://www.bu.edu/phpbin/ijahs/publications/?pid=389); Kapteijns' 1999 ISIM note: songs of praise for the leading women of early Islam, especially Faduumo (Fatima), sung by older women at a ceremony for a woman about to give birth; possibly related to pre-Islamic Oromo fertility songs — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2728244/view). A Somaliland Chronicle report (citing Awale 2013, Kapteijns 1995) adds the names *Xaawiyo Faadumo*, *Madaxshub*, *Abbaay Sittidey* (south), the etymology "most familiar explanation" from Arabic *sayyidāt*, a large *durbaan* played with two sticks by the session leader, circle seating, call-and-response, *jibbo* (spiritual climax), *taraaraysi* (pre-birth ritual), *raalliyo* ("the good woman") — [Somaliland Chronicle 2022](https://www.somalilandchronicle.com/2022/08/01/sitaad-the-somali-womens-traditional-spiritual-gathering/).
- A chapter "Somali Saar in the Era of Social and Religious Change" (2010) is listed in a spirit-possession volume (record page blocked by a browser check; details from the search listing only) — [relbib.de](https://relbib.de/Record/1759254959).

**Patriotic and political song**
- The catalogue records "Somaliyeey toosoo" as "Inno patriottico (1946, autore Cali Mire Cawaale)" sung by Qarshe — [Catalogo, DAT 2 id 12](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf). Qarshe wrote and composed "Qolobaa Calankeed" (1957) and the BBC Somali signature tune (per Wikipedia, sub-agent) — [Wikipedia, Abdullahi Qarshe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullahi_Qarshe).
- Harvard's Aryette collection post glosses *qaranimo* (Somali nationalism) as a frequent theme; the Waaberi Band track "Oktoobar Waatee? Waa Taayadii" (Said Mohamed Harawo) and Sangub's "Reject the Color of Imperialism" are regime-era political songs — [Loeb Music blog, Aryette post](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/loebmusic/2020/02/14/a-trunk-full-of-love-songs-somali-songs-1955-1991-the-maryan-aryette-omar-ali-collection/); [Ostinato Bandcamp](https://ostinatorecords.bandcamp.com/album/sweet-as-broken-dates-lost-somali-tapes-from-the-horn-of-africa); [Al Jazeera 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/19/somalia-races-to-save-radio-mogadishus-fading-archive).

### Inferences
- "Qaraami" has at least three documented senses: (1) a love-themed poetic type (Giannattasio's "tipo di gabay"), (2) Jama Musse Jama's period label for Somaliland's 1940s–mid-1960s song, (3) the everyday "old songs" sense (Geeska, Harvard). A glossary entry should state all three and date the musical sense as mid-20th-century usage.
- The balwo → heello → "new Somali song" sequence is consistent across Giannattasio, Johnson (via Wikipedia) and Goth; the only firm primary date is 1943 for balwo (Qarshe's own account in 1985) and 1949 for a political heello; the 1948 kaban date is Giannattasio's.
- Dhaanto's "late-19th-century Dervish revival" (Wikipedia citing Johnson) and Xaange's "first appeared in the 1930s ... developed from hurbo" cannot both be true as stated; both should be presented as competing accounts.
- "Shirib" and "batar" are northern/north-eastern dance-song names in the primary field sources, while "shirib" is also a southern Banaadiri verse form; any glossary that calls batar "southern" is unsupported.

### Gaps
- Johnson's own periodisation of the heello (his "heello A/B" and the heellooy refrain) could not be read: no accessible copy of *Heelloy* was found (Google Books/JSTOR not fetched; search budget exhausted).
- No source fetched gives a Somali-language description of the *heellooy heelleellooy* refrain's function beyond the book title and the "linked balwo" summary.
- The Arabic root of "qaraami" (presumably غرام) is asserted only in book blurbs; no dictionary or linguistic source was fetched.
- "Saylici", "wilwile", "jaandheer", "xoogweyn", "saddexley", "kabeebey" have no scholarly description beyond names and short journalistic or catalogue notes; dhaanto's rhythmic cycle is described only verbally (off-beat; 2/8 and 3/8 transcriptions of three couplets).

## Key question 2: Instruments (kaban, shareero, drums, clappers, horns, flutes, band instruments, playing verbs)

### Takeaway
Giannattasio's 1987 classification is the only systematic organology found: Somali thinking divides instruments not into the four Western classes but by the verb used to play them, *yeerin* ("make it sound": wind instruments and the camel bell) versus *tumid* ("strike": everything else, including the lyre and the lute); the kaban is dated to 1948 and the shareero is a six-string bowl lyre tuned to five pitches.

### Cited Findings
- Somali instruments "fall into two classes only": *yeerin* ("to let the sound of the organ be heard"; proper only for wind instruments such as *buun*, shell trumpet; *simbaar*, a kind of trumpet; *malkad*, horn) and *tumid* ("beat, strike": string instruments *shareero*, lyre, *kaban*, lute; membranophones *durbanno*, drums; the accordion and the organ; handclaps *sacab*; foot-stamping *jaan*; idiophones *shanbal*, wood clappers, *sharaq*, metal clappers); the wooden camel bell (*koor*) is an exception that is shaken; "sometimes the verb *garaacid* is used instead of *tumid*"; singing (*heesid*) covers the voice (*cod*) and the whistle (*foori*) of men and birds (*shimbiro*); from nomadic practice the terms *jiib* (multivocal singing) and *jaan* (stamping) have been transferred to modern bands "to distinguish the melodic from the rhythmic sections" — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 164 and Fig. 2](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984). Fig. 2 lists under HEESID: cod, foori, shimbiro; YEERIN: buun, turumbo, siimbaar, sagsoofan, koor; TUMID: shareero, kaban, fiyooliin, organ, biyaano, fiisarmooniyo, koox m. (bracketed LUUQ/JIIB) and shanbal, sharaq, durbaanno, jaan, sacab (bracketed JAAN).
- Giannattasio's 1987 classification table ("Classificazione degli strumenti musicali somali"): idiophones — *sacab* (handclap), *shanbal* (wooden clappers), *sharaq* or *biro* (metal clappers), *jaan* (ground stamped with the feet), *maqaar geel* (camel hide stamped with the feet), *baaf* (metal tub stamped with the feet), *shunuuf* (? body rattle), *teneg* (tin can struck with sticks), a metal plate struck with sticks, *koor* (wooden bell); aerophones — *gobeys* (wooden flute), a panpipe-type flute, *buun / caroog* (conch with apical or lateral mouthpiece), *gees* (side-blown horn, also *siimbaar*), *gees-goodir* (horn), *malkad / siimbaar* (trumpet), *zumari* (Bajuni double clarinet, Swahili), *parapanda* (five-hole oboe, Swahili); membranophones — *haan* (seasonal drum: the haan vessel covered with a skin), *durbaan* (tubular, single membrane), *durbaan* (sub-conical), *durbaan / yoome* (cylindrical, double membrane), *durbaan yar / jabbu* (small cylindrical), *nasar* (tubular drum with feet), "other drums and kettledrums (*masoonde, mokhoddon* or *tabuul, owlan, reeme* etc.)"; chordophones — *pango* (musical bow, Swahili), *seese* (tubular monochord fiddle, Swahili), *shareero* (symmetrical bowl lyre), *kaban* (lute "con tasti") — [Giannattasio 1987, p. 77](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736) (PDF: /bitstream/2307/736/1/).
- Instrument captions (Giannattasio 1987): *shanbal* are valve-shaped wooden clappers of *garas, gulaan, deegaan* wood, "diffusi in tutta la Somalia (eccetto alcune aree del nord)" as louder substitutes for *sacab* in dances such as *xayaad*; *sharaq/biro* iron clappers (in Gedo called *yaambooyin* = hoes; now made from car leaf-springs) accompany *Bullo, Gebley Shimbir, Beerrey*; *shunuuf* body rattle of vegetable fibre; *koor* wooden double-clapper camel bell made in sizes giving different pitches per camel type; *buun/caroog* conch trumpets of the coast and north-east announce events and formerly gave the battle signal; *gees* buffalo horn and *gees-goodir* antelope horn (south); *malkad/siimbaar* leather-covered wooden trumpet of the agricultural regions, ceremonial (births, marriages, the *istun* festival at Afgooye), cognate with Oromo *malakata*; *zumari* bone double clarinet of the Bajuni, like the Indian *zummara* and Yemeni *mazaamir*; *parapanda* five-hole oboe of Arab import; *durbaan/yoome* and *durbaan yar/jabbu* cylindrical double-headed wooden drums "probabilmente di derivazione araba", the larger used in the *Boorane* possession ritual, named *yoome* and *jabbu* (Swahili *chapu*) in the agricultural regions; tubular and sub-conical *durbaan*, *nasar* footed drum of *mukay* wood (Middle/Lower Shabeelle, Upper Juba, up to a metre tall), and *owlan* kettledrums of the river region; *shareero*: symmetrical bowl lyre (*xeero* = bowl), six strings tuned re4 re3 si3 la3 sol3 mi3, name "seems linkable to the Semitic root *knr*" (Hebrew *kinnor*, Arabic *kinnaara*, Amharic *kirar*, Coptic *cineera*, Greek *kithara*, Nubian *kissar*), used in the agricultural regions between the rivers and in the north at Berbera and in Togdheer, solo or accompanying song, and with *owlan* drums in the *Nuumbi* possession ritual; the chapter closes with a photo of "il poeta e suonatore di kaban Cabdullahi Qarshe" and of a National Theatre orchestra with violins, accordion, saxophone, guitars and drums — [Giannattasio 1987, pp. 79–89](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736).
- The kaban: "punto di partenza di questa nuova musica è stata l'introduzione, nel 1948, del liuto a tasti yemenita (kaban)" — the introduction in 1948 of the Yemeni lute gave a new musical form to the minor genres and led to the heello — [Giannattasio 1987, p. 75](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736); the catalogue describes Qarshe as "suonatore di kaban (liuto yemenita)" and a record of his 18 Dec 1984 scale demonstration ("Scala diatonica vs. scala pentatonica") carries the Somali keywords *gambuus, kaban* — [ArcAdiA 2307/1056](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/1056).
- Harvard Loeb: the oud is "called *kaman/kaban* in Somali" (Hudeidi post) and "*Kaman* (Somali) or *'ud* (Arabic)" (Aryette post) — [Loeb blog, tag Somalia](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/loebmusic/tag/somalia/).
- Geeska: the oud, "called *Kabanka* in Somali, entered British Somaliland from Aden"; Hudeidi also learned the violin (the article calls it "*Dhexyar*", unverified) with Abdi Handule and Hassan Nahaari; Qarshe is "often credited as the first person to bring the Oud into Somaliland" and sing in Somali while playing — [Geeska, "The making of Hudeidi"](https://www.geeska.com/en/making-hudeidi).
- Wikipedia (citing Abdullahi 2001): chordophones *kaban* (oud), *shareero* (lyre), *kinaandha* (lute), *madhuube* (thumb piano), *seese* (one-string fiddle); membranophones *nasaro, mokhoddon, masoondhe* (large), *reeme, jabbu, yoome* (small); aerophones *malkad, siinbaar* (flutes), *sumaari* (double clarinet), *fuugwo* (trumpet), *buun, muufe, gees-goodir* (horns); idiophones *shagal* (metal clappers), *shanbaal* (wooden clappers), *shunuuf* (vegetable ankle rattles), *tenegyo* (xylophone); roles *lahamiste* (lyricists), *abwaan* (songwriters), *odka* ("voice", vocalists); dance *ciyaar* — [Wikipedia, Music of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somalia); the Somaliland page repeats the list and gives *laxameyste* "melody maker/composer", *codka* "vocalist" — [Wikipedia, Music of Somaliland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somaliland). Note the conflicts with Giannattasio: *malkad/siimbaar* are trumpets, not flutes; *teneg* is a struck tin, not a xylophone.
- Modern band instruments in the field recordings: Qarshe's 1985 ensemble used kaban, violin, flute, bongos and *daf* (tambourine); Maclow Nuur (violinist-composer, Xamarweyne) was interviewed on Arab and Italian influences on 1950s Somali music and played an Arabic song with violin and *daf* — [Catalogo, DAT 2 id 13, DAT 3](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf). Ali Feiruz added guitar, violin and accordion in the 1960s (Wikipedia via sub-agent) — [Wikipedia, Ali Feiruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Feiruz). Walaalo Hargeisa added flute, violin and tambourine to the drum — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169). Waaberi's instruments are listed as vocals, guitar, oud and tambura — [Wikipedia, Waaberi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waaberi); 1970s–80s bands (Iftiin, Dur-Dur, Sharero) used keyboards, synthesizers, brass — [Al Jazeera 2017](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2017/8/18/uncovering-somalias-forgotten-music-of-the-1970s); Dur-Dur Band Int. credits drums, congas, claves, bass, keys, two guitars, saxophone — [Bandcamp](https://dur-durbandint.bandcamp.com).
- Djibouti: traditional instruments tanbura, bowl lyre, oud, small drums, reed flute — [Wikipedia, Music of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Djibouti).
- The *haan* is the largest nomad vessel, made of *qabo* or *argeeg* fibre with an awl (*mudac*); a 10–15 litre haan is used for churning; the empty haan is played as a drum with a wet kid skin held by two women — [Xaange 2014, ch. II §4, ch. IV §4](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).

### Inferences
- The shareero tuning given by Giannattasio (D4, D3, B3, A3, G3, E3) contains the pitch classes D E G A B, an anhemitonic pentatonic set, matching his general claim about Somali scales (KQ3); this is my reading of his caption, not his statement.
- Giannattasio's "liuto a tasti" (fretted lute) and the Somali keyword *gambuus* suggest the instrument first adopted was the Yemeni *qanbūs*-type lute rather than the modern fretless Arab oud; the sources do not say this explicitly, so the glossary should describe the modern kaban as the oud while noting the 1948 "Yemeni lute" wording.
- "Durbaan" is best glossed as the generic word for (cylindrical) drums, with *daf* for the frame drum/tambourine; the seed glossary's "frame or cylindrical drums" conflates the two.

### Gaps
- No Somali-language etymology for *kaban* was found; the Arabic *kamān* ("violin") proposal in the seed glossary is unsupported by any fetched source.
- *Shagal, sumaari, fuugwo, muufe, kinaandha, madhuube, tenegyo* appear only in Wikipedia's list (from Abdullahi 2001) and not in Giannattasio's field data.
- The *kabanle* ("oud player") term appears in none of the fetched sources.

## Key question 3: Musical structure terms (melody, voice, metre, alliteration, roles) and documented statements on scales, rhythm and tuning

### Takeaway
Giannattasio is the only scholar found who states the scale system in print: Somali melodies are "mostly anhemitonic pentatonic" (e.g. C-D-F-G-A), narrow in range, with binary rhythm in pastoral and ternary/cross-rhythm in agricultural repertoires and antiphonal or responsorial multivocality; Somali musical vocabulary centres on verbs (gabyid, heesid, ciyaarid, qaadid, yeerin, tumid, garaacid) and on *luuq*, *jiib*, *jaan*, *cod*, *miisaan*, with roles abwaan / laxamiiste (laxameyste) / codka attested only in Wikipedia-level sources.

### Cited Findings
- "Several distinctive traits of the Somali musical system": "a scalar system that is mostly *anemitonic pentatonic* (the melodies are constructed on a scale of five tones which lack semitones, for example C-D-F-G-A)"; "predominance of binary rhythm in the pastoral repertoires and of ternary and cross-rhythm in the peasant context"; "predominance of antiphonal (in alternative chorus) or responsorial (enunciation of the theme by a soloist vs. choral response) multivocality" — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 161](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984). The Italian 1987 version adds: ambitus "generalmente limitato, o di poco superiore, all'ottava" with little melisma; male vocal emission guttural, with low "lacerated" voice; female emission in a high register tending to head voice (falsetto) — [Giannattasio 1987, p. 76](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/736).
- "About 350 terms relating to music were gathered in the research", covering repertoires, instruments, modalities and techniques of performance; "the omnicomprehensive concept ... of music is a rather recent one"; "the term *muusika* refers principally to modern musical production performed with imported instruments (kaban, saxophone, guitar etc.)"; "the idea that Somali music was born in the forties might well depend ... upon a linguistic misunderstanding" — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 161 and n. 5](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984).
- *luuq*: wind instruments produce sound without manual action "only in its possible modulation (*luuq*)"; Fig. 2 brackets the melodic instruments as LUUQ/JIIB and the percussion as JAAN — [Giannattasio 1988, p. 164](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984). The expression "*shimbirtu waa gabyaysaa*" ("the bird is singing", lit. composing poetry) is meaningful; *qaadid* means "to perform a song (an already known piece)" rather than simply to sing — [ibid., nn. 6–7].
- Metre and music: Banti & Giannattasio, "Music and metre in Somali poetry", in R. J. Hayward & I. M. Lewis (eds), *Voice and Power* (African Languages and Cultures, Supplement 3), 1996, pp. 83–127: examines "how the metrical scansion system interacts with a melodic-rhythmic system" across kinds of Somali poetry; Somali keywords *maanso, muusika, miisaan, qaab*; full text not open access — [ArcAdiA 2307/5194](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/5194); their 1994 Italian paper "Cantare e contare nella poesia somala" (*Il verso cantato*, pp. 77–106) argues "the musical and the metric code are coordinated and synchronized in the audio chain" (keywords *xeerka muusikada, xeerka miisaanka*; open-access PDF listed) — [ArcAdiA 2307/1518](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/1518); Giannattasio, "Il tempo delle more" (2002, in *Sul verso cantato*, pp. 137–161) analyses the relationship between language and music in Somali poetry; subject heading *maanso::miisaan* — [ArcAdiA 2307/3067](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/3067).
- Giannattasio's own example: musical structures "interact with [the verbal ones] on various levels: rhythm, intonation, accent, segmentation", shown by the Ceerigaabo dance fragment (♪ ≈ 205) and by the three dhaanto couplets in 2/8 and 3/8 with triplets; he calls for a study of instruments' "etymology, organology, modality of construction and performance, regional distribution" and notes Somalia had no institutional music teaching — [Giannattasio 1988, pp. 165–167](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/handle/2307/984).
- O'Dubhda, "On the Musical Patterning of Sculpted Words: Exploring the Relationship between Melody and Metre in a Somali Poetic Form", *African Music* 8(3), 2009, pp. 97–116 (DOI 10.21504/amj.v8i3.1830) argues that all Somali poems can carry melody and that Burton first noted it; cites Banti & Giannattasio 1996, Johnson 1974/1996/1997 and Xaashi Dhamac 1976 *Miisaanka Maansada* ("The Meter of Poetry") — [African Music journal](https://journal.ru.ac.za/index.php/africanmusic/article/view/1830).
- Hirsi: the *dooro duureed aa* is "syllabo-quantitative" (fixed number of syllables in fixed order of weight), with matrix U U _ _ _ || _ U _ _ U (U = short-vowel syllable, _ = long, || caesura between *hoojis* and *hooris*); 18 of 24 identified Banaadiri forms are syllabo-quantitative; such forms ("chain meters" to Djibouti scholars, citing Orwin & Riiraash 1997) are rarer in nomadic poetry than the standard quantitative forms; poets may lengthen or shorten syllables to fit the metre (Axmad → Axmaad); the *kebed* weaving-song metre as given by Idris M. Cali is "W V W W W, W V W W, V W W V W W W" (W = long syllable or two shorts, V = short) — [Hirsi 2023](https://wardheernews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Aweys-Geedow-Nuur-Diinles-Ciyow-Biyaha-Keen-A-Case-Study-By-Ibrahim-Hirsi.pdf).
- Orwin, "Metre and 'extrametricality' in the geeraar" (in M. Orwin ed., *Papers from the Linguistics Workshop: Somali Language and Literature at the Hargeysa Cultural Centre, December 2015*, Dhaxalreeb series, Ponte Invisibile/Redsea, 2018) surveys earlier accounts of the geeraar metre and notes geeraar and gabay are metrically distinct and that there are various hees forms (PDF listed in search; not fetched — unora.unior.it returns 403) — [search listing](https://unora.unior.it//retrieve/handle/11574/199777/90124/Orwin%20-%202018%20-%20Metre%20and%20'extrametricality'%20in%20the%20geeraar.pdf). Farah Ahmed Ali Gamuute, *Coming of Age: An Introduction to Somali Metrics* (Ponte Invisibile/Redsea, 2018) is cited by Wikipedia and Hirsi — [Hirsi 2023 works cited](https://wardheernews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Aweys-Geedow-Nuur-Diinles-Ciyow-Biyaha-Keen-A-Case-Study-By-Ibrahim-Hirsi.pdf).
- Alliteration: Wikipedia Somali literature says Somali poetry has "obligatory alliteration" (Orwin 2011, "Alliteration in Somali Poetry") and calls it "*hikaad* (or alliteration)" with "an unwritten practice of meter" (Samatar 1982, p. 64) — [Wikipedia, Somali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_literature). The forms *higgaad* and *xarafraac* were not found in any fetched source.
- Roles: Wikipedia Music of Somalia — "lyricists (*lahamiste*), songwriters (*abwaan*), and vocalists (*odka* or 'voice')"; Music of Somaliland — *abwaan* "the poet or songwriter who writes the lyrics", *laxameyste* "the melody maker or composer", *codka* "the vocalist"; Music of Djibouti — lyricist (*midho*), songwriter (*laxan*), singer (*codka*), and *gouux* "a deep, sensual voice" — [Music of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somalia), [Music of Somaliland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somaliland), [Music of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Djibouti). These three pages contradict one another on *laxan/lahamiste*; Woolner's Somali abstract uses the spelling *laaxin* — [Cambridge repository](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/324232).
- *Hoobal* = artist: Hibo Nuura's track "Haddii Hoobalkii Gabay (If the Artist Lets You Down)" — [Ostinato Bandcamp](https://ostinatorecords.bandcamp.com/album/sweet-as-broken-dates-lost-somali-tapes-from-the-horn-of-africa); Magool's honorific "Hooyadii Fanka" (sub-agent, Wikipedia Magool) and Hargeisa's nickname "Hoygii Fanka" ("Home of Somali Arts") — [Wikipedia, Music of Somaliland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somaliland); "hoyga suugaanta" (home of literature) for Hargeisa — [Al Jazeera 2017](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2017/8/18/uncovering-somalias-forgotten-music-of-the-1970s).
- *Laashin* = poet/lead singer: Axmedey Cabdi Gaashaan directed the National Theatre folklore musicians as "laashin (poeta, capo cantore)" — [Catalogo, DAT 4](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf); southern poets ("laashin") composed and recited simultaneously (Luling 1996) — [Wikipedia, Somali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_literature).
- *Heesaayaal* (singers) and *laashinno* (poets) are used in the catalogue for the dhaanto self-presentation couplets; *alalaas/alalas* = ululation ("trilli di glottide"); *sacab* = handclaps; *lugo* = foot-stamping — [Catalogo, DAT 4, DAT 7 id 19, DAT 9](https://arcadia.sba.uniroma3.it/bitstream/2307/798/1/Catalogo%20dei%20documenti%20sonori%20sulla%20musica%20somala.pdf).
- Goth: *kasrad* = quick rhythmic handclapping; *majlis* = qaad-chewing sessions where men and women sang; *taxriig* = an Arab dance; *reer-magaal* = urban people — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169). Kapteijns: *heeso* (songs) are part of *suugaan* (literature); *soomaalinimo* (Somali identity), *miyi iyo magaalo* (countryside and city) — [WardheerNews interview 2014](https://wardheernews.com/?p=20456); [Kapteijns 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/discourse-on-moral-womanhood-in-somali-popular-songs-19601990/8B56054C3458B9805AA848CCC8079B06).
- Pentatonic claims in journalism: Iftiin/Sharero keyboardist Mahmud "Jerry" Hussen notes Somali music's use of pentatonic scales (PopMatters review of the Ostinato notes) — [PopMatters](https://www.popmatters.com/various-artists-sweet-as-broken-dates-2495378716.html); Al Jazeera: "Banaadiri: Mogadishu music based on Indian pentatonic scales" — [Al Jazeera 2017](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2017/8/18/uncovering-somalias-forgotten-music-of-the-1970s); Wikipedia: Somali songs are pentatonic (citing Abdullahi 2001) — [Wikipedia, Music of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somalia).
- Woolner's oud-apprenticeship article ("'Out of Time' and 'Out of Tune': Reflections of an Oud Apprentice in Somaliland", *Ethnomusicology* 65(2), 2021, DOI 10.5406/ethnomusicology.65.2.0259) is organised around three lessons each starting from "mistuning" in "a religiously contested postwar context"; the Somali abstract uses *cod doorsamay* and *laaxin*; full text not read — [Cambridge repository](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/324232).

### Inferences
- Giannattasio's anhemitonic-pentatonic statement (1987/1988) is the citable basis for the project's "pentatonic" descriptors; his caveat that it is "mostly" pentatonic, with the modern urban repertoire shaped by Arab, Indian and European influence (Qarshe's own recorded "diatonic vs pentatonic" demonstration, 1984), means modal/maqam material in qaraami should not be presented as purely pentatonic.
- The verb-based lexicon (yeerin/tumid/garaacid/qaadid/heesid/gabyid/ciyaarid) is better attested than the noun roles (laxamiiste, kabanle) and should be given priority in a glossary.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gives Somali names for scale degrees, modes, tuning or rhythmic cycles; Banti & Giannattasio 1996 and Giannattasio 2002 (which treat moras and musical rhythm) were not readable; Jama Musse Jama's 2023 transcriptions were not seen.
- *Laxan* vs *lahamiste/laxamiiste/laxameyste* needs a dictionary check (Zorc & Osman 1993 was not fetched); *halabuur*, *kabanle*, *qaafiyad*, *higgaad/xarafraac*, *jawaab-celin* have no fetched attestation.
- Orwin 2021 on hees vs maanso and Johnson 1996 "Musico-moro-syllabic relationships" were not accessible.

## Key question 4: History, institutions and key figures (radio, troupes, theatre, archives, venues, biographies)

### Takeaway
Dates for Radio Hargeisa differ by source (1941 transmitter, 1942 "Radio Kudu" test, 1943 per Wikipedia, 1945 first live Somali broadcasts, 1953 the name "Radio Hargeisa"); Radio Mogadishu dates to 1951; Waaberi grew out of the Radio Artists Association / Radio Mogadishu band and took its name after the 1969 coup; the National Theatre opened in 1967; the 1988 bombing of Hargeisa drove the hiding of tapes now held by Radio Hargeisa and the Redsea Cultural Foundation's Hargeysa Cultural Centre (opened August 2014); Hiddo Dhawr opened in 2014 (or 2013). Several key figures' dates are disputed (Qarshe 1994 vs 1997; Tubeec 1941 vs 1943; Omar Dhuule and Hussein Aw Farah undated).

### Cited Findings

**Radio Hargeisa**
- Geeska (Fathi Jama, 18 Oct 2025): 1941 a 100-watt transmitter at Taar-dheer for British police; 1942 tests as "Radio Kudu" (*Idaacadda Goodir*), relaying BBC news in Somali, Arabic and English; 1945 renamed "Hargeisa, Radio Somali", "the first live Somali radio broadcaster", power 600 W → 1 kW; 1953 permanent building and the name Radio Hargeisa; Guron-Jire (Mohamed Saeed) named as the first artist to sing on the station; Walaalaha Hargeysa ("Hargeisa Brothers") formed mid-1950s by Qarshe with Mohamed Saed Guron-jire and Hussein Aw-Farah; station songs: sign-off "Waan Hoyanayaaye, Habeen Wanaagsan", Qarshe's "Aqoon La'aani Waa Iftiin La'aan", Tubeec's Ramadan opener "Bishii Ramadaaneey"; Balwo movement "emerged in the mid-1940s" in Awdal, spread via Djibouti to Hargeisa — [Geeska](https://www.geeska.com/en/radio-hargeisa-and-rise-somali-modern-music).
- Wikipedia: established 1943 as the first Somali-language station (citing Johnson 1996 p. 58) — [Wikipedia, Radio Hargeysa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Hargeysa); Wikipedia Music of Somalia: "Radio Kudu" launched 1940, renamed Radio Somali the following year; Guroon Jire composed the first song broadcast — [Wikipedia, Music of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Somalia). The seed glossary's "founded in the 1940s" is safe; a single year is not.
- Archive: Radio Hargeisa staff hid "over 10,000 cassettes and master recordings" during the 1988 bombardment (Al Jazeera says "until 1987", an evident slip), some sent to Djibouti/Ethiopia, some buried; Shukri Ahmed (vice-minister of culture) leads restoration — [Al Jazeera 2017](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2017/8/18/uncovering-somalias-forgotten-music-of-the-1970s); Ostinato: the Red Sea Foundation's cassette archive of about 10,000 tapes in Hargeisa is "the largest Somali cassette collection in the world", from which 15 songs were chosen; Barre's state "effectively nationalized" music, private labels were virtually nonexistent — [Ostinato Bandcamp](https://ostinatorecords.bandcamp.com/album/sweet-as-broken-dates-lost-somali-tapes-from-the-horn-of-africa). An AFP report (syndicated; snippet only) gives Radio Hargeisa "over 5,000 reels and cassettes" and a project digitising ~14,000 cassettes at the Hargeisa Cultural Centre since 2021 — [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2583376/offbeat).

**Radio Mogadishu**
- Established 1951 under the Italian-run UN Trust Territory, first news in Somali and Italian; modernised with Soviet help after 1960; closed 1991; reopened 23 Aug 2001; digitisation of archives dating to 1951 began 2013 (page also carries a conflicting "1943 establishments" category) — [Wikipedia, Radio Mogadishu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Mogadishu).
- April 2026 UNESCO-backed assessment: ~45,000 tapes and reels, ~400,000 hours; >85 % playable; losses from 1992 fighting and a 2018 fire; digitisation began 2012 but only ~10 % done; aim to register with Memory of the World; archivist Abdiqadir Geedi Robleh: "the world's largest store of Somali language music, culture, dramas"; holdings include Mohamed Mooge Liban, Magool ("Oh Africa, still asleep"), Sangub — [Al Jazeera, 19 Jul 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/19/somalia-races-to-save-radio-mogadishus-fading-archive).

**Waaberi, Walaalaha Hargeysa, National Theatre, bands**
- Waaberi formed by members of the Radio Artists Association; renamed Waaberi ("sunrise") after the 1969 coup; government-backed as part of the National Theatre; toured Egypt, Sudan, China (Johnson, Grove), WOMAD 1997, North America 1998; past members Ali Feiruz, Hasan Adan Samatar, Magool, Maryam Mursal; continued privately into the 1990s — [Wikipedia, Waaberi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waaberi). The National Theatre page says Waaberi "grew from the Radio Mogadishu band and the General Daud military band" — [Wikipedia, National Theatre of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_of_Somalia). Goth: the Waaberi Group formed at Radio Mogadishu in the 1970s from Hargeisa veterans and a new generation and performed at FESTAC '77 in Lagos; Iftin was the Education Ministry's ensemble; Shareero and Dur-Dur were private — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169). Al Jazeera: Iftiin's student musicians were trained partly by North Koreans; Dur Dur formed 1981, privately owned; Sharero founded by Axmed Naaji; some bands were run by the police, army and penitentiary — [Al Jazeera 2017](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2017/8/18/uncovering-somalias-forgotten-music-of-the-1970s).
- Walaalaha Hargeysa: founded mid-1950s by Qarshe (Geeska, above); Qarshe founded "Walaalo Hargeisa" in 1955 and joined Radio Hargeisa in 1957 (Wikipedia via sub-agent) — [Wikipedia, Abdullahi Qarshe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullahi_Qarshe); Goth: formed shortly after Bons Bras staged the first modern play *Abiido* in 1955; Hussein Aw Farah was its composer and playwright — [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/25169).
- National Theatre of Somalia: built by Chinese engineers as a gift from Mao Zedong, finished and opened 1967; several state-linked bands including Waaberi performed there; among the first buildings destroyed in July 1991; reopened 19 March 2012; suicide attack 4 April 2012 (10 dead); 2013 China cooperation agreement for reconstruction; references include Andrzejewski's 1974 translation of *Shabeelnaagood* and his 2011 "Modern and traditional aspects of Somali drama" (*JACS* 23:1) — [Wikipedia, National Theatre of Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_of_Somalia). Hassan Sheikh Mumin (1931 Zeila – 16 Jan 2008 Oslo) wrote *Shabeelnaagood* (dated 1965 by Wikipedia) and its music, translated 1974 as *Leopard Among the Women*; Radio Mogadishu resident poet 1965–68; wrote and composed "Samo ku waar" (Somaliland anthem, 1997) — [Wikipedia, Hassan Sheikh Mumin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassan_Sheikh_Mumin).
- Kapteijns: the state put artists on its payroll and commissioned socially relevant songs through the two radio stations and the National Theatre — [Kapteijns 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/discourse-on-moral-womanhood-in-somali-popular-songs-19601990/8B56054C3458B9805AA848CCC8079B06).

## Key question 6: Orthography (1972 Somali Latin script and variant spellings)

### Takeaway
Standard spellings use c = /ʕ/, x = /ħ/, kh = /χ/, q = /q/, dh = /ɖ/, sh = /ʃ/, j = /dʒ/, doubled vowels for length, no p/v/z and an apostrophe for a non-initial glottal stop; the script was made official in October 1972 (21 October per the Cambridge note, as reported in search results).

### Cited Findings
- Letters and IPA: ' /ʔ/, b, t, j /dʒ/, x /ħ/, kh /χ/, d, r, s, sh /ʃ/, dh /ɖ/, c /ʕ/, g, f, q /q/, k, l, m, n, w, h, y; vowels a e i o u, long vowels written doubled (aa, ee, ii, oo, uu); diphthongs with y/w; tense/lax (front/back) vowel harmony and tone not marked; c was originally a superscript on typewriters; no p, v, z — [Wikipedia, Somali Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_Latin_alphabet); 21 consonants, five vowels, digraphs dh, kh, sh, formally introduced by the Supreme Revolutionary Council in October 1972; developed by Musa Haji Ismail Galal, B. W. Andrzejewski and Shire Jama Ahmed — [Wikipedia, Somali writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_writing_systems); [Wikipedia, Shire Jama Ahmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shire_Jama_Ahmed) (dates the rural literacy campaign to 1974–75). The Cambridge *Africa* note "Somalia introduces a national orthography" (1973) is the contemporary source (not fetched; the search snippet gives 21 October 1972) — [Cambridge Core PDF](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B71611FB0828E3A163C5159547DA1810/S0001972000073265a.pdf/somalia-introduces-a-national-orthography.pdf).
- Xaange's 1991 table gives phonetic descriptions and example words for each letter, including x as "Arabic ح unvoiced pharyngal fricative", c as "Arabic ع voiced pharyngal", q as "Arabic ق uvular plosive", dh as "voiced post-alveolar plosive", and distinguishes ten back and ten front vowel qualities that the orthography does not mark — [Xaange 2014, pp. 15–17](https://romatrepress.uniroma3.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/folk-xapu.pdf).
- Variant spellings met in the sources: balwo/belwo/belwa; heello/heelo/heellooy/hello; qaraami/qaraam/qaraamiga/"garam"; dhaanto/dhanto; buraanbur/buraambur/buranbur; kaban/kaman/kabanka/cuud/gambuus; shareero/shareeró/lumiya; shanbal/shanbaal/shambaal/shambal; sharaq/sharaaq/shagal; sittaat/sitaat/sitaad; durbaan/durbaanno/durbanno; mingis; saar/zar; wiglo/wilgo; hirwo/hurbo/xurbo; saylici/seylici/sayle/"Syalici"; batar/baatar; hobeey/hobeeya/hoobeeyo/hoobeeyaa/huwaaya; digri/dhikri/dikri/zikr; laxan/laaxin/lahamiste/laxamiiste/laxameyste; Qarshe/Qarshi; Sinimo/Siinimo/Shineemo; Guduuda 'Arwo/Guduudo Carwo; Hudeidi/Hudeydi/Xudeydi — compiled from the sources cited in KQ1–KQ4.

### Inferences
- Pre-1972 and Italian-era transcriptions (Giannattasio's catalogue uses standard orthography throughout; older works use "belwo", "Mogadiscio") explain most variants; English-language journalism drops c/x (Abdi, Hudeidi, Omar) and should be listed as variants, not as spellings.

### Gaps
- The 21 October 1972 date rests on a search snippet of the 1973 *Africa* note; the Wikipedia pages say only "October 1972".
