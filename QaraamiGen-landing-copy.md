## 6. Storytelling Sections (20) — short scroll-block narratives

1. **The Golden Age.** In the 1970s and 1980s, Mogadishu sang. Poets wrote verse, composers set it to the oud, and Radio Mogadishu carried it into every home. It was one of the great flowerings of African music — and almost none of it was ever written down.

2. **The Fragile Copy.** Most of what survives exists on magnetic tape recorded half a century ago. Tape decays. Players vanish. In many cases, a single aging cassette in a single drawer is the only copy of a song in the entire world.

3. **The Oral Inheritance.** For generations, Somali music passed from voice to voice, ear to ear. This is what made it alive. It is also what makes it fragile: a tradition carried only in memory is a tradition one generation from silence.

4. **What the Machine Hears.** QaraamiGen listens to a recording the way an elder would — following the melody, the scale, the rise and fall of the poetry — and does something no ear can do alone: it writes it down, permanently, note for note.

5. **From Sound to Sheet.** For the first time, a Qaraami melody can become sheet music. Not to freeze it, but to free it — so it can be studied, taught, and played by anyone, anywhere, for as long as there are musicians.

6. **A Map of the Melody.** Every tradition has an underlying grammar — its scales, its modes, its ways of moving between notes. We are drawing the first map of Somali musical structure, so the language of the music can be understood, not just heard.

7. **The Race Against Silence.** The people who remember these songs best are with us today. Not forever. Every month we wait, a little more knowledge slips beyond recovery. This project is, above all, a race.

8. **Not a Museum Behind Glass.** A preserved thing can still be a living thing. Our goal is not to seal Somali music away, but to make it more alive than ever — searchable, playable, and open to a new generation of musicians and scholars.

9. **The Return.** For families scattered across the world by war and time, these songs are home. To recover a recording is to reopen a room in a house they thought was lost.

10. **Beyond Preservation.** Understanding Qaraami melody is the first step toward understanding the Somali voice itself — a foundation for future research into the language, its poetry, and its sound.

11. **The Instruments.** The kaban — the Somali oud — carries much of this music's soul. We study how it was played, how it was tuned, how it answered the poetry, so the technique itself is never lost.

12. **A Civilization on Cassette.** Empires leave stone. Somalia's golden age left sound. We treat every reel and cassette the way an archaeologist treats a ruin — carefully, reverently, and with the knowledge that what we recover, we recover for everyone.

13. **Poetry First.** In Somali tradition, the word comes first and the melody serves it. To understand the music, you must honor the poetry inside it. Our work keeps them together, the way they were always meant to be.

14. **Open to the World.** What we recover will not sit in a private vault. It is built to be shared — with researchers, with teachers, with the children of the people who made it.

15. **The Long Project.** This is not a launch. It is a decades-long undertaking, closer to building a library than shipping a product. We are planting a thing meant to outlive us.

16. **Every Voice Counts.** Somewhere there is a cassette in a relative's home that exists nowhere else. Part of this work is finding those tapes before they're gone — and giving them a permanent home.

17. **What AI Is For.** The most human thing we can ask a machine to do is help us remember. Not to imitate the artists, not to replace them — to make sure they are never forgotten.

18. **Listening Like an Elder.** Our models are trained not just on notes, but on the way this music actually moves — its ornament, its breath, its feeling. Preservation without understanding is just storage.

19. **A Gift to the Unborn.** The clearest measure of success: a child born fifty years from now can learn the exact songs their great-grandparents sang, and know they were never lost.

20. **Why Now.** The tape is at the edge of its life. The people are at the edge of memory. The technology is finally ready. All three are true at once, for the first time — and maybe the last.

## Naming rules (apply throughout)

**The hero / proof excerpt melody is named `Beerdilaacshe`.** Wherever the page
refers to the specific melody being played and engraved — the player title, the
proof section, the score caption, alt text, and any share/OG metadata — name it
**Beerdilaacshe**, not a generic word like "Melody", "Excerpt", or "Sample".

Ready-to-use lines for the proof/hero excerpt:

- Kicker: `Beerdilaacshe — from a recording to a page`
- Title: `Beerdilaacshe, written down for the first time`
- Player label: `Beerdilaacshe — hero excerpt`
- Caption: `Press play. The engraving follows Beerdilaacshe note by note.`
- Alt text: `Sheet music of Beerdilaacshe drawing itself in time with the recording.`

**Use "Qaraami melody", not "Somali melody".** When describing the tradition's
melodic material, the precise term is *Qaraami* — it names the genre rather than
the whole country's music. "Somali music", "Somali musical heritage", and
"Somali-language research" stay as they are; only the melody phrasing changes.

Generic poetic uses of the word "melody" ("Every melody is a memory", "Memory is
fragile. Melody is stubborn.") are intentional and stay generic — do not
substitute a track name into them.
